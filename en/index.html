A structural grammar for governing complex systems in flux

Dynamic Sustenance Theory (DST) asks not "what a system is" but "how it sustains itself" — and turns that question into diagnosable, intervenable syntax. Applied to AI governance, climate, finance, and institutions.

The core move

Most governance asks whether a system complies with rules. That question assumes the rules are the right ones, and that stability is the goal.

DST starts one step earlier: it asks where the pressure is, and which constraint can still move.

The result is not a verdict but a structure — what state the system is in, and where intervention can actually take hold.

Static governance asksDST asks
What is the rule?Where is the pressure?
Who is accountable?Which constraint can still move?
Is the system compliant?Is the system still adjustable?

Existing complexity theory explains how systems evolve. DST works on the other question: how a system keeps itself alive.

Three structural invariants

Not a framework of layers or boxes — three relationships that hold in any system that sustains itself.

Force-Rule Coordination
The dynamic layer and the rule layer adapt to each other. Neither alone sustains a system.
Layered Asynchrony
The force layer and the rule layer are structurally out of sync. The response gap is permanent, not a temporary failure to be fixed.
Syntax Lock-in
When the rule structure freezes, the system loses the ability to re-weight rules as the dynamics shift. Even if a shock breaks it temporarily, the system returns to the locked state.

A general structural grammar for diagnosing where and how intervention can take hold.
